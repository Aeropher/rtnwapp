The honest answer: I built RightNow for me. I'm the target audience: someone who plans twelve hours of work into an eight-hour day, feels rubbish about the leftovers, and then does it again tomorrow.

But "chronic overcommitter" isn't a rare condition, so here's my best guess at who else might get something out of it.

## The problem it's aimed at

RightNow is about **overcommitment**: the habit of planning more than a day can actually hold. Most task apps quietly encourage this: the list grows forever and nothing ever pushes back. RightNow pushes back. You give it the hours you actually have, and when they're spoken for, it tells you the day is full.

## People I think it fits

- **Anyone whose to-do list is really a guilt list.** If your app of choice is where tasks go to age, the capacity bar and the refinement games are designed for exactly that pile.
- **People who are bad at estimating.** Every task carries a duration, and comparing your guesses with reality over time does teach you something about your own optimism.
- **Folks with ADHD or similar.** I've written [a separate post about this](/blog/rightnow-and-adhd/). The "what should I do right now?" framing and the hard limit on the day help me a great deal, and I don't think I'm alone.
- **Freelancers and students juggling several fronts.** Not because of any special features, but because the "does this actually fit today?" question bites hardest when nobody else is setting your schedule.

## Who it probably isn't for

If you already finish your list most days, or you want a heavyweight project-management suite with dependencies and Gantt charts and team dashboards, RightNow will feel too small for you. That's fine. It's meant to be small.

## See for yourself

I can theorise about audiences all day, but the quickest way to find out if it fits is to [try it free on the web](https://rtnw.app), no account needed to start. If it clicks, I'd love to hear about it.
